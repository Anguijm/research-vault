# Scenario 3 — Surge triage under tempo

## Setup

A three-ship surface action group took battle damage during a multi-day engagement. All three ships have been ordered to your Regional Maintenance Center for repair. The first ship arrives in 18 hours. The second arrives 36 hours after that. The third arrives 12 hours after the second.

Your RMC has the capacity to fully resource **one ship** at a time. You can run a degraded-throughput two-ship operation if you stretch your bearing-replacement, hull-welding, and electrical-trades teams thinner. A three-ship simultaneous operation is impossible at your current staffing. You have not yet been authorized additional contractor labor — the request is in but the approval timeline is uncertain.

The Naval Surface Force commander has asked you to recommend a triage approach. You have until 1200 today to send the recommendation.

## The decision

**Pick one triage approach. Defend it.**

A. **Sequential, full resourcing.** Take the first ship in fully, complete her repairs, then take the second, then the third. Highest quality work. Longest total time. Ships 2 and 3 wait at anchorage or alongside doing nothing for weeks.

B. **Parallel two-track.** Take ships 1 and 2 simultaneously with stretched teams. Complete both, then take ship 3. Faster overall. Stretched teams mean more rework risk and more incident risk on the live work.

C. **Parallel three-track with damage triage.** Bring all three ships alongside. Do *only the highest-priority repair item per ship* with what teams you have. The fast items get fixed across all three; the heavy items wait. Maximizes rapid partial recovery; gambles that the partial-recovery ships can be released to limited-mission tasking.

D. **Forward-deploy work to a partner facility.** Send ship 1 to your RMC (you fully resource her), send ship 2 to a CONUS sister RMC, request ship 3 be diverted to SRF-JRMC Yokosuka or a contracted commercial yard. You do less work; you accept that you do not control quality on the diverted ships.

E. **Wait for contractor labor authorization.** Hold all three ships at anchorage until the contractor-labor surge authorization arrives. Best work when you start it; worst time-to-first-repair if the authorization slips.

## The five variables

1. **Time-to-first-repaired-ship.** Operational commanders want a ship back in the fight. The sequence that gets ONE ship back fastest is operationally different from the sequence that gets all three back fastest on average.
2. **Total throughput.** Across the three ships, total work-hours-to-completion varies by option. A is highest-throughput per ship but slowest overall; B and C are more total throughput at a quality cost.
3. **Quality and rework risk.** Stretched teams make mistakes. Mistakes mean rework, which costs both time and credibility. Option B and Option C both increase rework risk.
4. **Strategic relationships.** Option D requires the Naval Surface Force commander to broker your partnership with a sister RMC or SRF-JRMC. Some of those relationships are healthy; some are not. The wardroom does not know which is which without asking.
5. **Authorization dependency.** Option E is conditional on contractor-labor surge authorization the wardroom does not control. The wardroom can lobby for it but cannot decide it. If the authorization slips a week, Option E becomes the worst option.

## Defender's prompt

Stand. In 60 seconds, defend the wardroom's triage approach. Name the operational priority you are optimizing for. Name the operational priority you are deprioritizing. The moderator will stop you at 60 seconds.

## After-action discussion prompts (for moderator)

1. **Time-to-first-repaired-ship versus total throughput — these are different priorities and usually point at different answers. Which did you optimize for, and what told you that was the right priority?** Surfaces whether the wardroom recognized the two priorities are distinct.

2. **Option D moves work out of your RMC entirely. Did the wardroom treat that as "losing the work" or as "the smart partnering call"?** Tests whether the wardroom over-identifies with their own production capacity at the expense of total operational outcome. This is a common bias.

3. **Option E is the wait-for-authorization option. The wardroom does not control the authorization timeline. Did anyone in the discussion argue for E, and what was the counterargument?** Often nobody picks E. Discussing why is more instructive than the choice itself — the wardroom should be able to articulate why "wait for permission" is not how operational repair works.

4. **The Naval Surface Force commander asked for a recommendation. What does the wardroom's recommendation tell the commander about how the RMC thinks under tempo?** Same as Scenario 2's prompt 4 — forces the wardroom to articulate their *signal to the customer*, not just their plan.

## Learning objective (for moderator only)

**Recognizing that the wardroom's job under surge is operational-outcome optimization, not own-RMC-utilization optimization.** Most wardrooms instinctively pick A or B because those keep the work at their own RMC. Option D — sending work to partners — is operationally rational under surge but cuts against the local-production instinct. The drill is teaching the wardroom that surge triage is not about maximizing your own RMC's hours; it is about getting ships back into the fight. The right choice in many real cases is D; the wardroom's job is to advocate for D even though it means less work for their own team. This is the cognitive flip the drill is designed to produce.
