# Scenario 2 — BDAT-to-BDAR handoff

## Setup

A frigate (FFG-XX) returning from a contested transit took **three separate hits** during a brief engagement. The Battle Damage Assessment Team (BDAT) embedded with the strike group spent eight hours on board assessing damage before the ship made it to your Regional Maintenance Center. The BDAT's report just landed in your wardroom inbox at 0530. The ship is alongside. The captain is asking what your repair sequence is by 0700.

You have one hour and 30 minutes to read the report, decide a repair sequence, and brief the captain. You have your full department-head wardroom assembled.

The BDAT report flags **four damage items**. Each is real. Each has a different repair profile. **You can only start two of them today.** The other two start tomorrow.

## The decision

**Pick the two damage items to start today. Defend the sequence.**

A. **CIWS mount** is non-functional. Self-defense is degraded. Repair scope: 36 hours, parts on hand, local welders sufficient.

B. **Forward berthing compartment** has structural cracking from a near-miss overpressure event. Cracks are below the waterline but not flooding yet. Repair scope: 5 days, requires shipyard-grade welders, parts on the way from Norfolk.

C. **Main electrical bus** has insulation damage from heat. The ship can operate but is one electrical event away from a propulsion-affecting casualty. Repair scope: 18 hours, parts on hand, requires de-energizing half the ship during work.

D. **Sensor mast** suffered RF-emitter damage to a primary search radar. The ship can fight without it but with degraded long-range warning. Repair scope: 4 days, requires a contractor field service representative from outside the RMC, FSR available in 48 hours.

## The five variables

1. **Mission-readiness recovery rate.** Each item's repair restores a different capability. CIWS restores self-defense. Berthing restores habitability (and structural integrity at depth). Bus restores electrical reliability. Sensor restores long-range warning. Which restoration buys back the most operational value first?
2. **Cascade risk.** The electrical bus (C) is a "one event away from a casualty" item. If it cascades, the ship goes from "repaired alongside" to "dead in the water alongside" and the schedule explodes. Cascade risk argues for early repair.
3. **External dependency.** Item B (berthing) and item D (sensor) have external dependencies — shipyard-grade welders for B, contractor field service representative for D. If you start them today you may *be stopped* tomorrow waiting on the dependency. If you start them tomorrow the dependency may arrive in time.
4. **Disruption to ongoing repair.** Item C requires de-energizing half the ship. Anything else you start today will be disrupted by that de-energization. Sequencing matters.
5. **Captain's preference.** The captain's hand-off note (not on the BDAT report itself) says the strike group resumes operations in 11 days. The captain has not stated a priority — that is your call. But your sequence will be the captain's first impression of your wardroom's judgment.

## Defender's prompt

Stand. In 60 seconds, defend the wardroom's two-item pick. Name the cascade risk you are accepting on the items you deferred. Name the dependency you are betting on. The moderator will stop you at 60 seconds.

## After-action discussion prompts (for moderator)

1. **The BDAT report gave you four items but no priority. Who actually owns the priority call — BDAT, the captain, or the RMC wardroom?** Tests whether the wardroom understands that the BDAT-to-BDAR handoff is incomplete — the report describes damage; the repair sequence is the RMC's call. This is the central pedagogical point of the BDAT-to-BDAR handoff scenario type.

2. **Item C (electrical bus) is the cascade item. Did you start it today, or did you accept the cascade risk? Why?** Probes risk tolerance. There is no wrong answer; there is only a defended answer.

3. **Item D (sensor) has an external dependency that arrives in 48 hours. If you started it today, you accepted potential idle hours on the team. If you started it tomorrow, you accepted that the FSR might slip. Which version of risk did your sequence choose?** Surfaces a common mistake — wardrooms often default to "start now to feel productive" without weighing the FSR-availability bet.

4. **What does the captain see in your sequence?** Forces the wardroom to articulate what their *briefing* looks like, not just their repair plan. The captain's first impression of the RMC matters for the rest of the engagement.

## Learning objective (for moderator only)

**The BDAT-to-BDAR handoff is incomplete by design.** BDAT describes damage; BDAR sequences the repair. The RMC wardroom owns the sequencing decision and cannot offload it to either the BDAT or the captain. Wardrooms that wait for "more priority guidance" from the captain are failing the drill — the captain is asking the RMC for the sequence, not providing one. The drill teaches the wardroom to take ownership of the sequencing call under incomplete information, which is the actual operational reality of a BDAR triage.
