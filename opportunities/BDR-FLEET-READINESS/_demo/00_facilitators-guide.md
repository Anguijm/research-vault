# Facilitator's guide — running a Level-1 flash drill

This guide tells a moderator how to run one Level-1 flash drill from the `scenarios/` folder. It assumes the moderator has not played the game before and is preparing to run it in front of a small group.

The whole session is 15 minutes. The whole guide is 5-10 minutes to read. The two should be roughly the same length so the moderator does not over-prepare and lose the spontaneity of the format.

## §1 — What this game is, in one paragraph

A Level-1 flash drill is a printed scenario card that puts a small team in front of one decision they would face in a real Battle Damage Assessment and Repair (BDAR) event. The team has five to ten minutes to pick an answer. Then one person from the team — the *defender* — has 60 seconds to defend the choice out loud. Then the moderator runs a brief after-action discussion using the prompts on the back of the card. That is the whole game. The cognitive skill the game is trying to build is **pattern recognition for which variables actually matter** when the team faces a real BDAR decision. The game does not have a single right answer.

## §2 — Who plays what

Three roles run a Level-1 flash drill. All three can be filled by the same group across multiple drills — players rotate.

- **The wardroom (2-6 people).** Plays the role of the Regional Maintenance Center (RMC) wardroom or staff cell. They read the scenario, discuss the five variables, and pick an answer together. They appoint one of their number as the defender.
- **The defender (1 person, from inside the wardroom).** Presents the wardroom's choice in 60 seconds. The defender explains *why* the choice is right given the five variables — not just *what* the choice is.
- **The moderator (1 person, outside the wardroom).** Reads the scenario aloud at the start. Runs the clock. Asks the after-action discussion prompts. Does not weigh in on whether the wardroom's choice was right.

The minimum viable session has three people total: two for the wardroom, one as moderator (with the moderator also serving as defender's audience). Two-on-one feels small but works. Five or six is the upper end before discussion gets diffuse.

## §3 — Timing

A 15-minute session breaks down as:

| Time | Phase | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00 - 0:30 | Setup | Moderator hands out the scenario card and asks everyone to read silently. |
| 0:30 - 2:00 | Setup read-aloud | Moderator reads the scenario aloud while players follow along. This is non-negotiable — reading aloud catches the players who skim. |
| 2:00 - 10:00 | Wardroom deliberation | Wardroom discusses, picks an answer, and selects a defender. The moderator does not participate. The moderator may give a 2-minute warning at 8:00. |
| 10:00 - 11:00 | Defense | The defender stands and presents the wardroom's choice in 60 seconds. The moderator runs a hard stopwatch — if the defender goes over 60 seconds the moderator interrupts. |
| 11:00 - 14:00 | After-action | Moderator runs the after-action discussion prompts on the back of the card. No more than 3 minutes total — the discussion is to surface *patterns*, not to debate the answer. |
| 14:00 - 15:00 | Close | Moderator names the cognitive skill the drill built and asks each player to name one variable they will pay more attention to next time. |

If the session goes long it goes long. The 15-minute target is to keep the cognitive load manageable, not to be a hard cap.

## §4 — Learning objectives

Every scenario card names its primary learning objective at the bottom. The moderator should not name it aloud at the start — naming the objective up front tells the wardroom the answer. The objective is for the moderator's awareness and for the after-action discussion. The defender's job is to articulate the choice *as if* the objective were the working theory; the moderator's job is to name it explicitly only at the close.

Across multiple sessions, the cognitive skills the game builds are:

- **Variable salience.** Which of the five variables actually drives the decision, and which are noise meant to look important.
- **Trade-off recognition.** When the five variables conflict, which trade-offs the wardroom is implicitly accepting.
- **Time discipline.** Real BDAR decisions are made under time pressure. The 5-to-10-minute deliberation window is short on purpose.
- **Communicating under constraint.** The 60-second defense forces the defender to compress a multi-variable decision into one sentence of rationale. That is the skill an actual operational briefing requires.

The game does NOT try to build technical BDAR knowledge. A player who does not know what a destroyer's reduction gear is can still play the game — the card explains what each variable means. The game is about the decision process, not the technical content.

## §5 — How to run the after-action discussion

Each scenario card has 3-4 after-action discussion prompts on the back. Run them as questions to the wardroom, in order. Do not let any one prompt run more than 60-90 seconds.

The pattern of a good prompt sequence is:
1. **What did you weigh most heavily?** Surfaces which variable the wardroom treated as load-bearing.
2. **What was the strongest argument against your choice?** Tests whether the wardroom considered the alternative.
3. **What variable did you spend the most time arguing about?** Often reveals that the wardroom argued about a variable that did not actually drive the decision — the variable-salience lesson.
4. **What would change your answer?** Probes the decision's robustness.

The moderator should not provide answers to the prompts. The point of the discussion is for the wardroom to hear themselves talk through the trade-off — that is where pattern recognition gets internalized.

## §6 — What the moderator should NOT do

- Do not say which choice is "right." There is no single right answer in any Level-1 scenario. There are better and worse defenses of a choice; the defender's job is to give a defense the wardroom can stand behind.
- Do not add facts not on the card. If a player asks "but what about the weather?" the moderator answers: "the card tells you everything you have. If weather is not listed, treat it as not a factor."
- Do not pause for technical explanations beyond the variables on the card. If a player asks what "host-nation contracting friction" means, the moderator can paraphrase the card's definition once and then move on.
- Do not let one player dominate the wardroom deliberation. If one player is doing all the talking, the moderator calls on the quietest one with a direct question after 5 minutes.

## §7 — Running multiple drills in a session

If you have an hour with the same group, do not run one drill for an hour. Run three drills back-to-back from different scenarios. The pattern recognition compounds across drills. After three drills the wardroom will start to notice that the same VARIABLE structure shows up in different decision contexts — and that is the level-up moment the game is designed for.

If you only have 15 minutes, run one drill. Do not skip the after-action discussion to make it shorter. Cut the wardroom deliberation phase to 5-6 minutes instead.

## §8 — When this guide is wrong

This guide assumes a moderator who is new to the game. Once a moderator has run 5-10 drills, the moderator should feel free to deviate. The two things that are NOT negotiable:

1. The 60-second defense. If the defender talks for 90 seconds, the cognitive constraint that makes the game work has broken. Stop the defender at 60 seconds even if it feels awkward.
2. The moderator does not name the learning objective at the start. If the wardroom knows what skill the drill is testing, they will perform the skill instead of learning it.

Everything else in this guide can be flexed once the moderator has the pattern.
