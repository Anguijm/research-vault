## Key content

### 1. SIMA San Diego stands up today (1430, Building 3116, Naval Station 32nd Street)

This is the central thread of Robinson's keynote and the reason the talk happens at MegaRust 2026 in San Diego on 2026-06-04 specifically.

**Background.** The CNO issued a December 2025 mandate to NAVSEA to "stand up a SIMA" to help establish combat-ready forces. **SIMA = Shore Intermediate Maintenance Activity** (operator-confirmed 2026-06-05). Whisper rendered the acronym as "SEMA" and "CIMA" interchangeably in the raw transcript — both are mishearings of SIMA.

**Chain of direction:**

- CNO → NAVSEA (Admiral Downey, Vice Admiral).
- Vice Admiral Downey → CNRMC (Rear Admiral Daniel L. Lannaman, Commander, Navy Regional Maintenance Center). Whisper rendered the surname as "Landman" / "Laniman"; correct spelling confirmed via NAVSEA RMC public page (Lannaman relieved Rear Admiral William Greene in March 2025).
- CNRMC → SWRMC (Robinson) and MARMC, with direction to split off their Production Departments (Code 900) and establish SIMAs.

**Execution at SWRMC.** Six-person transition team taken offline, GS-14 in charge. Told: "you will stand up SIMA." Cut through organizational pushback. 100-page MOA governs the separation.

**Today's ceremony, 1430 local, Naval Station 32nd Street, Building 3116:** CNO, Vice Admiral Brendan McLane (Commander, Naval Surface Forces — Whisper rendered as "McClain"), Vice Admiral James P. Downey (NAVSEA Commander), and Rear Admiral Daniel L. Lannaman (CNRMC Commander) attending. ~30-minute event, curtain pull on the building, SIMA stood up.

**SIMA leadership:**

- **Commanding Officer:** Captain Bill Albert. (Whisper rendered the surname inconsistently — "Albert" in some segments, "Daubert" in one — treat as Bill Albert.)
- **Executive Officer:** coming from Naval Station Great Lakes, where he was CO of the training center.
- **Training department head:** Captain Carl Brandel, O-6.
- **Nuclear training officer** (Brandel's subordinate, exact title not on tape): female, "incredibly talented" per Robinson, helping stand up the training department.
- LDOs (Limited Duty Officers) being brought in.

**Personnel transition today:** all military personnel at SWRMC moving to SIMA, except ~14 Engineering Duty Officers, 1 supply corps officer, and 1 SWO XO. "A little bit shy of 900" sailors going across.

**Timeline:**

- IOC stand-up was 1 June 2026 (three days before the ceremony — IOC and ceremony are decoupled).
- Today (2026-06-04): physical ceremony, badge / ball cap distribution, two-command operating identity begins.
- June 2029: "hairball" unraveling complete — full separation of comptroller, IG, JAG / Office of Counsel functions, etc.

### 2. Rear Admiral Daniel L. Lannaman's four SIMA principles

From Robinson's slide:

1. **Sailor self-sufficiency**
2. **Consolidate military personnel**
3. **Prioritize mission effectiveness**
4. **Identify and address gaps**

Robinson's argument: SWRMC has been operating against these four principles inside its Production Department for 21 years already; SIMA is a restructuring of an existing capability, not a new build.

### 3. SWRMC history (the second-stand-up framing)

Robinson framed the SIMA stand-up as the second major reorganization of his career at SWRMC:

- **2004:** SWRMC stood up from five separate commands ("shotgun wedding"). Robinson was on the team that built the initial structure. A lot of organizational resistance; "got out because it was great leadership."
- **2006:** Military stand-down ordered (not SWRMC's choice). Two leaders named:
  - **Bill Riswick** — deceased ("I know Mr. Riswick has passed"). Supported keeping SWRMC buildings and funding for support contractors and some civil service to keep capability alive.
  - **Jim Ockenbach** — Robinson's predecessor as Executive Director. Robinson still sees him routinely. Drove the same retention-of-capability argument.
  Robinson credit: "their decisions back in 2006 has made it possible for us to stand up SIMA San Diego here in San Diego with a strong foundation going forward."
- **2010:** SWRMC evolved off the early-Navy "crude lean" methodology toward value-focused excellence. Stopped stripping shops of tools and spare parts; started focusing on what value the shop was trying to deliver to the customer.
- **2026:** SIMA San Diego stands up — second separation, with a 22-year-old SWRMC organization splitting Code 900 out.

Robinson's Toyota production system / lean-manufacturing framing: SWRMC organized shops into "product families" rather than the standard hull-mechanical-electrical (HM&E) layout. Welding shops embedded inside other shops where welding isn't the primary trade but is needed regularly. Explicit critique of the cosmetic Navy lean implementation that strips tools.

### 4. SWRMC's four funded RMC missions

1. **Contract Management Oversight (CMO)** — project teams overseeing depot-level contracts on the waterfront.
2. **Assessment** — Code 200 / engineering department. Goes out and does assessments on ships (combat systems, tanks, varied scope between CNO Availabilities).
3. **Fleet Technical Assistance (FTA)** — Robinson's "Maytag repair people." Sit and wait for ship calls. Two modes: on-board technical assistance (physical visit, can be Pier 6 or Diego Garcia) and distance support (computer-mediated). Robinson noted distance support is the emphasized methodology but has the same frustration as any remote-vs-in-person service. Concrete example: flying personnel into Souda Bay because a ship's SeaWhiz isn't working "and they really need that SeaWhiz for all that crap Iran's throwing at us."
4. **Intermediate Maintenance** — "straight stick" — the traditional SIMA-style mission. Moving to SIMA San Diego today.

### 5. Expeditionary Maintenance — Pacific battle-damage-repair pre-staging (cross-reference: SRF-JRMC adjacency)

Robinson named this as currently an unfunded mission area but called out as "really, really cool." This is the most SRF-JRMC-relevant content in the keynote.

**The framing.** Tenders for the surface force do not exist. Maybe in the 30-year shipbuilding plan, maybe modify some auxiliary ships, but for the foreseeable future there is no hardcore tender plan. So PACFLEET leadership has SWRMC pre-staging "shops in the box" — outfitted maintenance containers — in the Pacific to do repairs on ships and prepare for battle damage repair on ships in the Pacific.

**Numbers:** Approximately **50 containers** total. Not a full set yet — "handfuls of them." Adequate for the single-ship 2-week CNAPs (Combatant Logistics / NAVSUP-style deployments) being done currently.

**Operational pattern.** Containers either:

- Stay where they are pre-staged, with sailors / civilians flown out from SWRMC to activate them, OR
- Move to an "austere location" to set up shops on demand.

**SRF-JRMC integration on tape:** Robinson said SWRMC is **"actually sending project teams for contract oversight, working with SRF Japan, which of course is the area of maintenance coming under out there to support these things."** That is a direct on-tape statement of SWRMC-to-SRF Japan operational coordination on the expeditionary-maintenance mission.

**Driver named:** Pat McDermott. Robinson credited him as the person driving this mission area, with senior leadership ("the four-star also got a boot on his rear end") backing the push. Disruptive (~20-30 people out of SWRMC shops per deployment) but the right thing per Robinson.

**Operational realism example:** "Freon here in the States ain't the same as Freon overseas. And they don't let us ship Freon from the States overseas. So we've had to go back and modify air conditioning units to swap out Freon and stuff like that. That's the stuff that would really suck to learn if you had to do this real time with kinetics flying around."

### 6. Advanced Manufacturing (Robinson's term; he rejected "additive")

Robinson explicitly chose "advanced manufacturing" over "additive manufacturing." Three technologies in his definition:

1. **CNC** — "we still know how to make stuff out of metal."
2. **Additive Manufacturing (AM)** — plastic, composites, metal.
3. **Reverse Engineering** — Robinson called this "the Achilles' heel."

**State of AM at SWRMC:**

- "None of these ships right now are additive manufacturing. That will change. I have no doubt about it."
- A laser deposit additive manufacturing machine — Robinson kept calling it "**EMG Maury / GMG Maury**" — almost certainly **DMG MORI** (the brand). Months of effort just getting it operational. Inside machinists are trying to develop weld processes, which is not their expertise. Robinson's instinct: "Why the hell aren't we getting the engineers over here and doing this? It's because everybody's not focused on this stuff."
- A welder told Robinson the AM machine completed in minutes a pass that would have taken him hours.
- Pressure cycle: Vice Admiral Brendan McLane (Whisper rendered as "McClain") pushes Captain Kerosich (Robinson's CO, see Whisper artifact note below) every week — "why can't we make that? Why can't we do this? Why can't we do that with additive manufacturing?" Kerosich brings the question back to Robinson's team.

**Reverse engineering as the bottleneck.** Robinson: engineers need real tools (more than calipers and micrometers) to take a broken part with no drawings and produce drawings that can drop into CAD for CNC manufacturing.

**Three AM example projects on Robinson's slide:**

1. **5-inch gate valves and larger** — supply system out of seats, throwing valves away was wasteful. Now SWRMC laser-deposits material onto the eroded seat and cuts it off with CNC. ("Lay down a deposit on those seats and then cut it off with the machine tools in the CNC.")
2. **Shaft coupling bolts** — exotic, not readily available, sometimes ship is on the docks (Robinson's word, paraphrased) and the bolts are missing. SWRMC reverse-engineered them, made plastic models, now producing experimental versions on CNC.
3. **Bridge crane motor on an amphib** — crane down for an extended period. OEM contacted, said the motor was replaced by a different model with different mounting holes. Reverse engineers went on-site (catwalks), mapped out the existing motor and the new motor's footprint, produced a "drill here, drill there" plan to mount the new motor.

### 7. Production examples Robinson highlighted (slide-supported)

- **Scuttle from the powder coating shop** — "better than what's being delivered in new construction." Robinson said he was discussing it with **Mark Lattner** this morning. (Cross-reference: Mark Lattner is the NAVSEA 05P SES who gave the Anti-Rust Program briefing on Day 2 in clip 7.)
- **Diver team (sailor + civilian)** — does underwater husbandry in the San Diego area, flies routinely to Singapore to manage battle gear out there, and used to fly to Bahrain ("not so much anymore") to be there with **MARMC** (Mid-Atlantic Regional Maintenance Center) at the same time — operator-confirmed 2026-06-05 that Whisper rendered MARMC as "Mark" and that what Robinson actually said was a co-located or co-owned relationship (not "covert" as Whisper rendered it). The on-tape framing is two RMCs informally coordinating their dive-team deployments to overlap in theater.
- **Hydraulic test stand** — mounted on a piece of wood. Robinson cited as an example of experimentation in the shops.
- **Corrosion control on RSDs** — small crawlers on flight decks. Subject to constant salt spray. SWRMC's process has dramatically improved over 20 years — now coming back to the shops at 5-year turnarounds in good condition.
- **Watertight doors** — Robinson cited an industry partnership (Whisper rendered as "ACL," identity not worth chasing per operator) starting around 2012. Doors used to come back rotted at the gasket. Welder rebuilding the whole gasket frame made no cost sense but had to be done because doors weren't available in the supply system. Now doors are coming back in much better condition.
- **Super RBOC** — gear exposed to constant salt spray on weather decks. SWRMC powder-coats, paints, and refurbishes electronics, then sends back out.
- **Honesty moment.** "The biggest problem I think we've got when it comes to corrosion protection is the ships and our ship sups, they know our capabilities, and they will just resend it back to us, either every CNO availability or every other CNO availability, whether it needs it or not. There's a little bit of a waste on that. Over the years what we found was arguing takes more time than just doing it again."

### 8. SIMA philosophy — "you need both effectiveness and efficiency"

Robinson's argument against the framing he said was coming from non-maintainers during the SIMA stand-up: "We focused on efficiency too much over the last 20 years, and not on effectiveness. And we need to focus on effectiveness and get rid of the efficiency." Robinson called that "a bunch of bull crap." His position: both matter, both are needed for an organization that can be flexible to fleet needs without being wasteful.

His specific fear about SIMA going forward: it becomes the "easy button" — "I've got a hole in a pipe. I need a team down there in 15 minutes." Disruptive to a production environment, and "you will burn out your safety. ... a lot of stomach acid, a lot of gray hairs that come with it." His ask of Captain Albert is to protect against that pattern.

### 9. Admiral Belisle anecdote (industry-engagement framing)

Robinson recounted Admiral Belisle visiting SWRMC for "a Voice F.R.E.2 thing" (Whisper artifact — possibly "VFR-2" or a voice-of-the-fleet-related visit; not legible from context). After the diesel engine shop walk-through, Belisle asked: "Why doesn't the Navy know about this?" Robinson took it two ways — partly that SWRMC is bad at selling its story, partly that the Navy doesn't communicate about its sustainment work. Robinson's framing: "We're supporting the type commander. We're supporting the ships. The communication about this needs to get better."

## Q&A

### Q1 — Andy (unidentified, "Andy is that you?" from Robinson)

"Is there a faster track" for getting new development to the ships, especially additive manufacturing? Why does it take so long to flow new tech through SWRMC to the fleet?

**Robinson:** Acknowledged "old timers like John Robinson — I will throw myself in there first — have this romantic notion of how the Navy was when we were young." Argued the SIMAs of the 80s and 90s weren't actually perfect either. The fix is pairing young military energy with older civilians who know how to do things and want to mentor — that pairing degrades when organizations get split. His fear: that pairing won't survive the SWRMC-to-SIMA split.

### Q2 — John Mark Woods (local AMPP section chair)

Thanked Robinson for the history lesson and for opening SWRMC's doors to AMPP. Said the AMPP tour "planned for an hour and we went intense 5" — they really walked the shops. Asked: where can industry help SWRMC stand up SIMA or address longer-range tech gaps?

(Whisper rendered AMPP as "ASDA" / "ASDee" in this exchange. Operator-disposition needed.)

**Robinson:** Cited an industry partnership (Whisper rendered the company as "ACL" and the lead as "Brad") — "Brad showed us how to do world-class corrosion prevention powder coating as opposed to pretty cosmetic powder coating, which is what we're doing." That kind of industry partnership matters, but contracting it is hard. Robinson's broader point: SWRMC is bad at reaching out and asking for help, partly out of pride. Encouraged AMPP and industry to be a sounding board. (Identity of "ACL / Brad" not worth chasing per operator 2026-06-05.)

### Q3 — Dr. Maureen Foley (cross-reference: Day-1 clip 6 speaker)

**Foley introduced herself: "Hi, I'm Dr. Maureen Foley. I run the composite components."** Robinson responded immediately: **"Yes, ma'am, you gave us a new machine this week."**

**This is a direct on-tape confirmation that NSWC Carderock's composite-fabrication equipment was delivered to SWRMC during MegaRust week.** The equipment Foley referenced on Day 1 (heated wire bending tables, sheet stock material, waterjet cutting equipment) per her "Composite Work Cell Concept" slide listing SWRMC as one of six active deployment sites is now physically at SWRMC.

**Wrinkles surfaced in the exchange:**

- The machine "hasn't really been exercised" yet at SWRMC (Foley's statement).
- The machine was "missing an arm or something like that" (Robinson) and "that back and forth about that arm never got resolved" — until apparently it has been (Robinson: "and we have another one. Super.")
- Foley asked how to get more involved in advanced manufacturing pitches at SWRMC ("opportunities to pitch new technologies").

**Robinson's answer.** Foley should practice "intrusive leadership" — call SWRMC, drop by, ask whether the machine has been used and whether problems have been found. SWRMC's "PET team" (process engineering team — likely; Whisper-rendered without expansion) is "amazing in the shop" — three master-trained people who care about the product — but they don't necessarily know who Foley is or how to connect to her at the right level. Three-year military rotation churn (~1/3 of military personnel turn over each year) makes process continuity hard. Robinson's invitation: don't wait for SWRMC to reach out; come in, work the building.

**An audience voice (unclear speaker)** then added: "Reach out to the ED. That also helps." (ED = Executive Director = Robinson.)

**Cross-reference value for the SRF report.** Foley's slide on Day 1 listed SRF-Japan among the six active deployment sites. SWRMC and SRF-Japan are both on that list. The fact that the SWRMC delivery happened during MegaRust week — and that SWRMC hadn't exercised the machine yet, and that there were unresolved equipment-completeness issues — is directly relevant to the "what equipment has actually arrived at SRF-Japan" question in the SRF "when I get home" follow-ups. The SRF-Japan delivery may be in a similar state.
