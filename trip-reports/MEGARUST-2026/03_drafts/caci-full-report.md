# MegaRust 2026 — CACI Internal Full Trip Report (v0.4)

I attended ASNE MegaRust 2026 in San Diego across 2026-06-02, 2026-06-03, and the morning of 2026-06-04 (Day 3 ran 0800–1030 for me; I left before the afternoon agenda). Ten substantive sessions captured via audio plus slide photos where available.

---

## 1. Trip context and what changed

MegaRust 2026 is the annual ASNE-sponsored Navy-and-industry conference on ship corrosion and preservation, with 20+ years of cross-organization continuity. This year's conference added a cross-service DOD Corrosion Programs Panel, replacing the 2025 DOD Corrosion Conference that didn't happen — corrosion-program leads from Navy aviation, Marine Corps, Air Force, and Army all in one room. Conference Chair Dave Zilber framed the panel as the program-level pickup of the lapsed DOD corrosion-conference thread.

The trip surfaced real shifts in how NAVSEA and the broader DOD are reorganizing sustainment work — shifts that change where money flows and who has authority over what. They group into three areas:

1. **Policy.** NDAA §345 (the new statutory mandate I brought into the trip as context); Vice Admiral Lewis's Day-2 PAE acquisition reform keynote.
2. **Programs.** The Anti-Rust Program (Mark Lattner's Day-2 brief) post-launch; Surface Team One / SURFMEPP Pacific build-out (Holman's Day-1 session) with reach into NSWC Port Hueneme by Day 2; **SIMA San Diego standing up Day 3** with ~900 sailors moving over from SWRMC's Production Department; **SWRMC's Pacific-theater expeditionary maintenance container program** with SRF Japan named as the Area Maintenance Commander in the Western Pacific.
3. **Technical authority.** Mark Ingle (NAVSEA 05P2) and the coatings Technical Authority Pyramid; the split between combat-systems coatings (NSWC PHD) and HM&E topside coatings (NSWC Carderock plus PCoE); SWRMC's advanced manufacturing program with reverse engineering as the named Achilles heel.

This report walks each in turn, then surfaces cross-trip patterns, the industry presentations at the Day-2 robotics technical track, follow-up items, and the action items I'm carrying after the trip.

## 2. Policy

### 2.1 NDAA §345 — Technology Enhancement for Surface Ship Maintenance

Conlan Hsu (NSRP) cited §345 at the opening of the Day-1 NSRP SPC panel meeting as the policy backdrop for the conference. I had also brought it into the trip as pre-trip context. Public Law 119-60 §345 was signed 18 December 2025. The Secretary of the Navy is required to investigate and adopt advanced surface-ship-maintenance technology, with drone-based inspection for uncrewed structural surveys specifically prioritized. Adoption is mandatory. The section text names no deadlines, no dollar amounts, and no specific programs. Per Holland & Knight's FY26 NDAA analysis: "Requires the Secretary of the Navy to investigate and adopt advanced technologies that improve Navy surface ship maintenance and prioritize drone-based inspection for uncrewed structural surveys."

**Why §345 matters across the trip.** Hsu's opening reference set §345 as the announced policy backdrop, and multiple program activities I documented across the three days line up with it — the Anti-Rust Program, NSWC Port Hueneme's patent-pending NDI work (thermography and fiber-optics), PCoE's equipment deployments to the Regional Maintenance Centers, the Edify pierside / wet-berth magnetic-crawler NDI from the Day-2 robotics track, and SIMA San Diego standing up to consolidate sailor-self-sufficiency capability. §345 reads as the standing policy reason behind program-level activity at the conference and beyond.

### 2.2 PAE acquisition reform (Vice Admiral Dave Lewis, ASNE President)

Lewis opened Day 2 with a direct fix for a 70-year problem: the Navy organizations that buy ships have never been the same organizations that have to maintain them later. That separation is why corrosion-prevention work has been chronically underfunded — the buyer has no incentive to pay for what only the maintainer pays for. He told the audience:

> "The buyer of the stuff is not the same organization. It's the maintainer of the stuff. Part of the reform is to create portfolios where the buyer and the sustainer are the same human being. ... Within that PAE, they own the contracting, they own engineering, they own management. Everything is in one place."

His direct instruction: "Go find your PAE and make nice with that person — they now are the solution to our corrosion issues."

**What this means for CACI.** The new Portfolio Acquisition Executive (PAE) is the new center of gravity for sustainment funding decisions. For any CACI sustainment pursuit, the relevant PAE is now the named relationship-development target. Whether the reform actually changes how money flows is an implementation question, but a Vice-Admiral-level acknowledgment of the underfunding pattern is itself a new posture worth noting.

## 3. Programs

### 3.1 Anti-Rust Program (Mark Lattner, NAVSEA 05P SES)

Post-launch as of January 2026. Two parallel tracks:

- **CCAT (Corrosion Control Assistance Teams)** — the established sailor-training-and-tools model, 16 years old, nine locations worldwide.
- **SWARM Teams (Swift Warship Action on Rust Mitigation)** — contractor crews doing production preservation work during short availabilities, working areas sailors can't easily reach (running rust at deck edges, surfaces inaccessible during deployments).

**Numbers since January 2026:** 85 ship availabilities worked, about 60,000 square feet of preservation done, more than 3,000 additively-manufactured parts delivered to ships. Lattner: "more AM components on Navy ships than everyone else combined."

**SWARM Teams alongside sailors, not replacing them.** On Day 1, Andrew Sheets (Marine Corps) and Ian Shannon (Navy aviation) made a structural observation: once contractors take on preservation work, it becomes hard to give that work back to sailors later because sailors stop learning the trade. On Day 2, Lattner addressed this directly — the Anti-Rust Program structures SWARM Teams to work alongside sailors rather than take work over from them.

**Industry Day:** 14-15 July in Norfolk VA. POC is Josh Chapman at NCMS, Josh.Chapman@ncms.org. I'm not attending personally.

### 3.5 SWRMC's Pacific-theater expeditionary maintenance container program (SRF Japan as Area Maintenance Commander)

The most BDR-FLEET-READINESS-adjacent content in the trip.

**Robinson's framing.** Tenders for the surface force don't exist and aren't in the near-term Navy shipbuilding plan. So SWRMC has been pre-staging approximately **50 expeditionary maintenance containers** ("shops in the box") across the Pacific theater for battle damage repair on surface ships. Containers can stay pre-positioned or be moved to an austere location, activated by SWRMC sailors and civilians flown out from San Diego. Currently the program supports single-ship two-week deployments. The mission is unfunded. **Pat McDermott** is driving the program — Robinson didn't expand on his organizational position.

**On SRF-JRMC's role.** Robinson said SWRMC is "actually sending project teams for contract oversight, working with SRF Japan, which is, of course, the Area Maintenance Commander out there, to support these things." So SWRMC project teams coordinate through SRF Japan in its Area Maintenance Commander role on the container deployments. The exact division of work between SWRMC and SRF-JRMC on the container mission and the related Battle Damage Exercises isn't visible from Robinson's keynote — an internal SRF question to clarify before deciding on engagement.

**Operational constraints worth knowing.** Robinson named the Freon issue: US Freon used in air conditioning units is not the same as Freon used overseas, and you can't ship US Freon overseas. So SWRMC modifies AC units to swap Freon before deployment. There's a broader pattern of items in these containers that don't ship via standard airfreight — calibration gases used for gas-free devices are "literally bad gas" and can't move by air either. Planning for this mission means working those constraints up front.

**What this means for CACI.** Operationally adjacent to the BDR-FLEET-READINESS work. See §6 for cross-references. Pat McDermott is the Navy-side counterpart for any CACI BD posture touching Pacific battle-damage-repair pre-staging or surface-ship-maintenance technology adoption under §345.

### 4.3 Active coatings spec updates

**Standard Item 009-032 FY-28 Change 1** — published 31 March 2026. NAVSEA adopted 49 of 79 industry-proposed changes (about 60%). Top five changes per Ingle's Day-2 brief:

1. **A new zinc-rich paint option** (MIL-DTL-24441 Type III Formula 159) added to Table 2 Lines 1B/1C. 85%+ zinc by weight, cathodic protection at scratch defects. Four qualified suppliers.
2. **A new biocide-boosted foul-release antifouling** (MIL-PRF-24647E Type IV). Alternative to copper-based antifouling. Up to 7-year service life.
3. **A streamlined waterfront process for two-coat interior topcoats** (paragraph 3.1.16).
4. **A new magazine and ammunition hold coating note** (Note 12) — MIL-DTL-24441 Type IV Formula 152 white epoxy for magazines holding saluting charges.
5. **Fluidized-bed powder coating expanded** to airtight doors, watertight doors, hatches, and scuttles (Table 2 Line 41).

**MIL-PRF-16173F.** New Class 8 "Persistent" preservatives added — for spaces where it's not practical to install a coating (rudder interiors, bilge keels, voids). Publication held August 2025 to late May 2026 by a NAVAIR-vs-NAVSEA format dispute. NAVAIR objected to a new Type/Class/Grade format that would have forced expensive updates to downstream NAVAIR documents; the resolution kept the Class/Grade format and the first five coating identifiers similar to legacy 16173E. Andy Vazquez (NAVSEA) showed Class 8 in voids on a ship.

**MIL-PRF-24596 mold/mildew update.** Six-week test in a petri dish with a 1mm clear-zone requirement around the coated coupon. Demonstrated on a carrier — controlled mold growth in a magazine for 5+ months. Industry letter went out February 2026 inviting re-qualification.

**MIL-PRF-24712B Amendment 1** — powder coating spec update. Published 28 December 2025 (ten days after §345 was signed). Color requirements moved from the cancelled FED-STD-595 to the active SAE AMS-STD-595. Industry letter went out March 2026 inviting re-qualification.

**Oxsol reformulation.** Ingle: "Oxsol is parachlorobenzyl trifluoride." Oxsol-100 is a non-VOC solvent used in MIL-PRF-24635F Type V coatings. California listed it as a Proposition 65 carcinogen in 2019. The South Coast Air Quality Management District is changing Rule 1106 to phase it out. NRL is leading the reformulation work through ESTCP project WP21-5154 (PI is James Tagert at NRL); the qualification package is in progress.

### 4.4 Combat-systems coatings vs. HM&E topside coatings

Two different sustainment domains, two different specs, two different program offices:

- **NSWC Carderock** handles HM&E (hull, mechanical, electrical) topside corrosion — deck edges, hull plating, painted surfaces. The framework is Standard Item 009-032 and MIL-PRF-23236 epoxy + MIL-PRF-24635 polysiloxane chemistry. PCoE under Fazende manages the spec updates. Mark Ingle is the technical authority.
- **NSWC Port Hueneme** handles combat-systems corrosion — AEGIS, NSSMS, RAM, AEM/S, UNREP equipment. The funding pathway is different (In-Service Engineering Agent and the On Ship Refurbishment Program), and the coating spec is different (MIL-PRF-85285, the aviation-derived polyurethane that's the standard topcoat on all U.S. Navy and Marine Corps aircraft per MIL-STD-2161).

**What this means for CACI.** Any CACI BD work touching surface-ship-maintenance coatings should distinguish which sustainment domain it touches. The two coating-spec lanes don't fully overlap, and the program offices, funding paths, and access points are different.

### 4.5 SWRMC's advanced manufacturing program — reverse engineering is the named Achilles heel

Robinson rejected "additive manufacturing" as the framing in his Day-3 keynote. His definition: **advanced manufacturing = CNC + AM + reverse engineering**. CNC is the mature capability; AM is being learned; **reverse engineering is the Achilles heel** because engineers need real tools (more than calipers and micrometers) to take a broken part with no drawings and produce CAD-ready drawings for CNC manufacturing.

The named machine is a **DMG MORI** laser-deposit additive manufacturing system, months into commissioning. Inside machinists are trying to develop weld processes — work Robinson said should be engineers'. Vice Admiral McLane (Naval Surface Forces) pushes Captain Kerosich (Robinson's CO at SWRMC) weekly on AM progress.

**Three example projects Robinson cited:**

1. **5-inch gate valves and larger** — laser-deposit material onto eroded seats, machine off with CNC. Replaces the throw-away-the-valve pattern in the supply chain.
2. **Shaft coupling bolts** — exotic, not readily available, sometimes ship is docked and the bolts are missing. SWRMC reverse-engineered them, made plastic models, now producing experimental versions on CNC.
3. **Bridge crane motor on an amphib** — crane down for an extended period. OEM contacted, said the motor was replaced by a different model with different mounting holes. Reverse engineers went on-site (catwalks), mapped out the existing motor and the new motor's footprint, produced a "drill here, drill there" plan to mount the new motor.

**What this means for CACI.** Robinson's named reverse-engineering gap is real and at the ED level. If CACI has a reverse-engineering-tooling capability for Navy maintenance organizations, the timing is favorable. (My SRF-side play involves Amanda Keach, SRF-JRMC AM Program Manager, connecting to Robinson — that's a separate channel from any CACI-direct engagement.)

## 5. CAPT Duldulao keynote — combat-systems corrosion as a warfighting problem

CAPT Richard Duldulao (NSWC Port Hueneme, Chief Engineer MIL) delivered the Day-2 keynote, "Material Degradation: Threat to Surface Ship Combat Operations."

**Framing.**

> "When a part of the [combat] system degrades, [the] kill chain potentially weakens. ... persistent, quiet, and relentless adversary. It doesn't sleep, doesn't need a supply chain, and operates on every single ship in the fleet 24 hours a day, 7 days a week. That adversary is the maritime environment, and its weapon of choice is, as we all know, corrosion."

**Cost data from his slides:**

- $14M per SPY-1 array replacement. Three arrays per DDG.
- $450K per FCS MK 99 director replacement, plus the alignment and collimation work that follows.
- $500K per AEM/S resurfacing (per Tim Tenopir Q&A).

**Named hulls with FCS director corrosion replacements during recent availabilities:** USS Wayne E. Meyer (DDG-108) and USS Cole (DDG-67).

**FY25 INSURV data:** about 900 combat-systems deficiencies attributed directly to corrosion, more than 660 of which actually degraded mission capability.

**On RAM.** "A few seized train bearings in the active fleet" — RAM launchers physically unable to slew. A multi-million-dollar launcher that can't track or engage an incoming threat.

**AEM/S structural concern.** Severe localized corrosion in the crow's nest area that ship's force can't easily reach. The bottom band carrying the entire mast weight is severely corroded with interior surfaces inaccessible. Dissimilar-materials interfaces drive the corrosion. Tenopir mentioned in passing that "some future LPDs don't have AEM/S anymore."

**On Ship Refurbishment Program (OSRP) for NSSMS and RAM.** 30-year cost-savings table from Duldulao's slide: $25.9M per CVN, $25.1M per LHA/LHD, $21.9M per LPD/LSD compared to the Depot Overhaul alternative. OSRP cycle is 3-6 weeks vs. Depot Overhaul's 1 year.

**Samet's "data-to-action gap" framing** is the thread tying all four presentations together: current inspection robots collect data, but they don't assess, plan, execute, or report on the remediation work. Closing the gap requires linking the four capability categories.

**Jeff Apt cited a CBO report on 14 years of dry-dock yard-period overruns** as the data foundation. Edify's value pitch (per the operational workflow that makes sense for a Navy waterfront): deploy the magnetic crawler during a non-docking maintenance period (ship pierside), produce a hull-thickness inspection report, and use the report to plan the next docking availability's scope — well ahead of the ship actually docking. The value isn't last-minute pre-docking inspection; it's giving the maintenance planners enough lead time to size, fund, schedule, and procure for the work against hard data. The report is CBO publication 61940, "Maintenance Delays for Conventional Navy Ships" (December 2025) — 14 years of Navy schedule data (October 2010 – September 2024), 225 DDG-51 docking and non-docking overhauls, 20%–150% schedule overrun vs. 2012 class-plan estimates. Directly usable in any CACI brief arguing for non-docking-availability hull inspection capability that feeds docking-period planning, or for surface-ship-maintenance technology adoption under §345.
